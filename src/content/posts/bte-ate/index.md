Traditionally, we’ve labeled historical periods using **B.C. (Before Christ)** and **A.D. (Anno Domini)**, or the more modern, religion-neutral **B.C.E. (Before Common Era)** and **C.E. (Common Era)**. But what if we step back and think: *Is there a single date that truly demarcates the biggest shift for humanity?* 

From a **systems thinking** perspective, one could argue that **the advent of the internet** fundamentally changed the world to a similar—or possibly greater—extent than countless royal reigns or religious milestones. When we talk about how life *really* transformed in our collective consciousness, the gap between *pre-internet* and *post-internet* is staggering.

---

## Why the Internet is a Tectonic Shift

### 1. Information Access
In the “before times,” if you wanted to explore a new woodworking technique or compose music collaboratively with someone halfway around the globe, you faced steep hurdles—libraries, snail mail, expensive phone bills, or specialized courses. After the internet became mainstream:
- **Instant Research**: Entire repositories of how-to videos, open-source designs, and niche user forums became available 24/7.  
- **Global Collaboration**: Real-time jam sessions, code pairing, or design critiques with people across continents became routine.

### 2. Economic Models
Pre-internet, the biggest businesses were tied to tangible goods or real estate. Now, massive companies thrive primarily on *data, software, and online services*. Consider social media giants or search engine behemoths—these wouldn’t exist at scale without the connectivity backbone of the web.

### 3. Cultural Impact
How we socialize, consume media, and share personal milestones has changed drastically. The once-monolithic broadcast model (a few TV networks or record labels) gave way to user-generated content and streaming platforms. *Memes* have become a cultural currency, and news cycles refresh by the minute.

---

## The Internet as a Historical Marker

### Parallel to the “BC/AD” Approach

- **B.I. (Before Internet)**: A time of limited global connectivity, slower data exchange, and localized knowledge.  
- **A.I. (After Internet)**: Instant, borderless communication with a never-ending, fast-paced digital flux.

The argument isn’t about dismissing the religious or cultural significance of the BC/AD or BCE/CE systems—it’s about recognizing that from a technological and societal viewpoint, the internet triggered changes in human behavior on a scale that rivals or even dwarfs many epochal events.

### Other Possible Markers

Of course, the internet isn’t the *only* watershed moment. People sometimes point to:
- **Industrial Revolution**: Mechanized production and urbanization.  
- **Printing Press**: Mass literacy and the spread of knowledge.  
- **Nuclear Age**: The harnessing of atomic power (and the existential threats it brought).

But the internet stands out for its ubiquity, speed of proliferation, and direct impact on day-to-day life. Almost *every* sector—education, commerce, healthcare, entertainment, personal relationships—has been touched.

---

## A Systems View: How the Internet Changed Our Behavior

1. **Feedback Loops**  
   From social media “likes” to viral hashtags, we’re seeing real-time feedback loops that amplify certain topics or behaviors. This shapes public opinion, markets, and even political landscapes more quickly than ever before.

2. **Lower Barriers to Entry**  
   Pre-internet, aspiring creators or entrepreneurs faced huge barriers—agents, publishers, physical storefronts, etc. Today, you can spin up a website or channel overnight. This democratization fosters innovation but also unleashes an ocean of noise—leading to new challenges of discoverability.

3. **Global vs. Local**  
   Pre-internet societies were more regionally siloed. Now, local events can resonate globally in minutes, collapsing geographic and cultural boundaries. Conversely, hyper-niche communities can emerge around the most specific interests—something nearly impossible in a purely offline world.

4. **Data-Driven Societies**  
   The shift toward big data, AI, and analytics hinges on near-instant exchange of information. The internet fuels that exchange, evolving how we design products, govern cities, and even maintain our personal health records.

---

## Why This Matters

If we anchor our historical lens to “before internet” and “after internet,” we can more readily see:
- **How education must adapt**: The volume of instantly accessible information renders memorization less critical; instead, synthesis and critical thinking become paramount.
- **Shifts in social structures**: Social media, remote work, and digital communities redefined what it means to be “together.” 
- **Economic transformations**: E-commerce, gig economies, cryptocurrency—these developments *require* an internet-centric viewpoint.

Historians of the future might ask how digital identity, global interconnectivity, or the attention economy emerged. Using “Before Internet” (B.I.) and “After Internet” (A.I.) as markers could guide that storytelling.

---

## Potential Critiques

1. **Cultural & Religious Significance**  
   The BC/AD system (or BCE/CE) is deeply ingrained in global traditions, documents, and our collective psyche. Overhauling it might be seen as erasing important cultural milestones.

2. **Inequality Gaps**  
   Not everyone has equal access to the internet. Large parts of the world still lack reliable connectivity. So framing everything as B.I. and A.I. might oversimplify the digital divide.

3. **Multiple Technology Waves**  
   The internet was revolutionary, but so were the printing press, electricity, and the smartphone. It’s arguable which wave made the most profound difference or if we should consider a blended timeline of multiple tech milestones.

Despite these critiques, it’s hard to deny that the internet sparked a fundamental shift unlike anything else in the last century, and we’re still living through its aftershocks.

---

## Embracing a Post-Internet Mindset

As an engineer, musician, and woodworker, I’ve felt the personal influence of B.I./A.I. arcs:
- **Software**: Before the internet, coding was often solitary, reliant on books or localized circles of experts. After the internet, open-source platforms and global communities exploded, accelerating learning and project development.
- **Music**: Recording, distribution, and collaboration used to be limited to physical mediums and pricey studios. Now, you can create and share tracks from a laptop, reaching audiences worldwide instantly.
- **Woodworking**: You no longer need to apprentice in a single location to pick up advanced techniques. Video tutorials, digital CAD tools, and online maker communities broaden skill sets dramatically.

Reframing history around the internet acknowledges this epic inflection point. It helps us contextualize our current moment—where data travels at light speed, and human connections leap across borders—and it urges us to build more equitable, sustainable structures for the future.

---

## Final Thoughts

The idea of measuring history in reference to the internet might sound unconventional, but so did the Gregorian calendar once upon a time. Every major shift in our collective timeline stems from pivotal changes in how we live, communicate, and innovate. 

While the internet isn’t the *only* world-altering event, it’s undoubtedly a titan that reshaped humanity’s trajectory. Recognizing “Before Internet” and “After Internet” as chronological waypoints might help future generations appreciate the massive cultural and technological pivot we’re still navigating. 

After all, if you can learn how to build a complex piece of furniture from a master craftsman in another country, or collaborate with a musician 8,000 miles away in real-time, you’re inhabiting a world that’s profoundly distinct from anything that existed a few decades ago. **We’re truly in the A.I. era**—not artificial intelligence (though that’s another conversation), but *After Internet*.
